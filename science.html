01 — for the curious

The missing fundamental illusion.

When a sound source produces a fundamental pitch (the lowest frequency in a harmonic series), the auditory pathway can often reconstruct that pitch even when the fundamental itself is absent — inferring it from the harmonics that remain. This is the Missing Fundamental Illusion (MFI). The reconstruction involves a complex interaction across multiple levels of the auditory system, from brainstem structures including the superior olivary complex through auditory cortex, likely with frontal involvement in voice identification. For people with impaired H1 perception, this pathway does not perform the reconstruction reliably — the failure point within the pathway is not yet well characterized.

In everyday conversation, intonation melody rides on the fundamental pitch. At social distances, that fundamental attenuates faster than the harmonics above it — arriving degraded or absent at the listener. What arrives at the listener's ears is the harmonic stack — without the melodic information it once carried. The brain attempts reconstruction. Whether that reconstruction is accurate varies significantly between individuals.

Resonero addresses this by delivering intonation melody back into the room acoustically — before reconstruction is necessary.

Why it matters for communication: Intonation melody is the primary carrier of emotional meaning in speech. It's what distinguishes a sincere "thank you" from a sarcastic one; what signals a question from a statement; what carries warmth, frustration, care, or boredom. Without it, words are ambiguous.

— a broader context

Intonation doesn't just matter to humans.

Most animals that communicate emotionally through voice do so almost entirely through what might be called the music of speech — the rise and fall of pitch, the pace, the volume, the rhythm. Dogs are a good example: their range of distinct sound units is limited, but their pitch and timing variations carry rich social and emotional information.

Humans have something unusual: a vocal tract capable of producing a very large range of distinct sound units, combined with the neurological architecture to control them with precision. This combination — rare among animals — is what makes spoken language possible. But it may have led to a subtle neglect of the older, more ancient channel.

Producing distinct sound units (phonemes) requires precisely shaping the vocal tract to selectively amplify and suppress harmonics. This means that phoneme-rich human speech is, in a sense, harmonically disrupted — the clean harmonic series that would make pitch reconstruction easy is continuously reshaped by articulation. Dog vocalizations, which carry almost no phonemic load, have a comparatively richer, less disrupted harmonic series.

Preliminary observations from our lab suggest that people who don't reconstruct fundamental pitch reliably from human speech do better when harmonic richness is higher. This may help explain an informal observation: some people who struggle to read emotional tone in human conversation appear to read dogs' emotional states quite well. If so, it may be because dog vocalizations offer the kind of harmonic richness that makes emotional pitch information legible — the signal these listeners are good at reading, without the disruption that phoneme production introduces. These are preliminary observations, not established findings, and we are actively investigating.

Research using brain imaging suggests that dogs process intonation and word meaning separately, in different brain regions — much as humans do — and that reward centers activate only when both are consistent with praise. This research was conducted with familiar voices; whether dogs generalize prosodic reading to unfamiliar voices is less established. Since dogs are frequently at social distances from their owners, they may also be working from an attenuated H1 signal, relying on harmonic envelope and rhythm rather than fundamental pitch directly.

Some people are notably better at reading a dog's emotional state than others. This individual variation may reflect the same differences in prosodic perception that Resonero addresses in human conversation.

Prosodic modulation of the voice appears to be widespread across animal species — reviewed across primates, mammals, songbirds, and insects by Filippi (2016), who hypothesizes it paved the way for the development of linguistic prosody in humans. Osmanski & Wang (2015) show that complex pitch perception mechanisms are shared between humans and marmosets, suggesting these capacities are phylogenetically old.

Trained vocal performers — actors, singers, orators — develop techniques that partially compensate for H1 attenuation at social distance. Projection, resonance, and breath support training all increase the harmonic richness and amplitude of the voice, making intonation melody more accessible to listeners even without H1 being reliably present. This is part of what distinguishes a trained stage voice from a conversational one. Resonero addresses the gap for the rest of us: the untrained, natural conversational voice that most people use in most of their daily interactions.

Linguists distinguish two layers of spoken language: the distinct sound units that make up words — called segmental features — and the pitch, pace, rhythm, and stress that carry emotional and relational meaning — called suprasegmental features. Educational systems generally focus much more on the segmental than the suprasegmental: on words, rather than how they're said. The pitch, pace, and emotional tone that carry meaning underneath the words — the music of speech — are left largely to chance, or to the performing arts. Resonero addresses exactly that older channel.

02 — for the technical

Signal and mechanism.

The human voice produces a complex harmonic series rooted at the fundamental frequency F0 — the instantaneous pitch produced by vocal fold vibration. F0 typically spans 85–255 Hz in adult speech, with the full harmonic series extending several kilohertz above it.

H1 (F0) propagates spherically — omnidirectionally — while higher harmonics are progressively more directional, delivering a greater proportion of their energy toward the listener. Combined with the inverse square law reduction in intensity with distance, H1 attenuates faster relative to the harmonic series above it. The result: at 2–4 meters, the harmonic energy reaching the listener retains the overtone structure of the voice but has lost much of the H1 energy that defines intonation melody.

The IA mechanism

Intonation Augmentation captures near-field vocal signal (where F0 is present) via a pickup positioned close to the speaker, processes it to isolate and amplify intonation melody, and re-delivers it into the room acoustically via a speaker. The re-delivered signal is ambient — not directional or headphone-bound — so all participants in the room receive it simultaneously.

The processing chain targets F0 identification, intonation melody extraction, and level-calibrated acoustic reproduction. The goal is perceptual transparency: the delivered signal augments rather than replaces the room acoustics, and should not be identifiable as a separate source by listeners.

03 — for the neuroscience-minded

Nascent circuits, individual variation, and polyvagal contrast.

The broadcast voice observation: FM radio announcers use close-miced techniques that capture very high levels of low-frequency energy — far more than Resonero delivers. This broadcast voice is universally described as soothing and calming. Polyvagal Theory proposes that the autonomic nervous system responds to low-frequency acoustic features as potential threat signals, and that prosodic features of voice — particularly those signaling social safety — modulate this response. If low-frequency sound intrinsically triggered stress, broadcast voice would be alarming. It isn't. Our hypothesis extends this: the issue may be specifically unidentified low-frequency sound. Intonation Augmentation delivers intonation melody, enabling source identification — and an identified, socially-safe source does not trigger threat response.

Our working model proposes that a subset of people possess neural circuits primed to process intonation pitch information that are not reliably stimulated in face-to-face social speech — because H1 doesn't survive propagation from a human mouth at social distances. These circuits are stimulated in other contexts: close-miked broadcast voice, film, podcasts, intimate conversation at close range. The deficit is specific to social speech, not to auditory capacity generally.

The circuits are not absent or damaged — they engage in the media contexts above. What's missing is reliable engagement in social speech. Resonero provides exactly that: H1 at conversational distance, in the room, continuously. The same stimulus that film and radio deliver incidentally, delivered deliberately into the context that has always lacked it.

A historical note. Reliable H1 reproduction in media is a post-1950s phenomenon — dependent on magnetic tape, FM broadcast, and high-fidelity loudspeakers capable of full-spectrum reproduction. Before that, electrical recording and AM radio had significant low-frequency rolloff that stripped H1 much as social distance does. Before electrical amplification entirely, the only contexts delivering reliable H1 were intimate distance and some close acoustic instruments. The neural circuits in question have had a media stimulus pathway for roughly 70 years — unevenly, depending on access, habits, and cultural context. For most of human history, face-to-face social speech was essentially the only H1 source available, and it fails at social distance.

Individual neural architecture variation

The reliability of MFI reconstruction varies substantially between individuals. This variation is not fully explained by standard audiological measures (pure-tone audiometry, for example, does not assess MFI reconstruction reliability). The illusion page includes a direct demonstration — Eric's MFI video lets you hear whether your auditory pathway reconstructs H1 reliably.

Variation in reconstruction reliability likely has multiple sources: genetic factors affecting auditory pathway organization at one or more stations, developmental history of acoustic exposure, and neurological variation across the pathway — from brainstem through cortex — in how periodicity pitch is extracted from harmonic information. The specific locus or loci of failure for impaired MFI perceivers are not yet well characterized in the literature.

Contrast with polyvagal approaches

Polyvagal Theory (Porges) proposes that the autonomic nervous system regulates social engagement partly through the middle ear muscles — specifically the stapedius and tensor tympani. In a ventral vagal (socially safe) state, these muscles actively attenuate low-frequency sound, making mid-frequency vocal prosody more perceptible and social engagement easier. In a defensive state, the muscles relax, low-frequency environmental sounds become more salient, and the system orients toward threat detection rather than social connection. The directionality matters: it is not that low frequencies trigger anxiety — it is that anxiety causes low frequencies to become more perceptible, because the suppression mechanism is withdrawn.

Polyvagal-based auditory interventions (e.g., Forbrain and related approaches) target this nervous system state directly, via bone conduction delivery of modulated sound intended to exercise the middle ear muscles and restore ventral vagal tone.

Our approach targets a different layer: intonation melody identification, not nervous system state. We hypothesize these address distinct populations and distinct mechanisms — not the same challenge via different paths. By delivering identifiable intonation melody — a social safety signal — Resonero may facilitate ventral vagal tone, which then activates the middle ear muscles, which then suppress the low-frequency ambient sounds that would otherwise register as potential threats. This is consistent with the sleep observation from early users: when intonation melody is present, previously ambiguous low-frequency percepts become identifiable as human-sourced and non-threatening.

A practical self-assessment for anyone considering polyvagal approaches: think of the stereotypical smooth jazz announcer — calm, measured, close-miked, unhurried, with boosted low frequencies including H1. Does that voice feel soothing or anxiety-producing? For most people it is deeply calming. If it is calming for you too, your nervous system is likely already regulating well in response to identified vocal sources; your challenge may be H1 reconstruction reliability in face-to-face speech, which is what Resonero addresses. If that voice feels activating or uncomfortable, that may suggest a nervous system state in which low-frequency signals register as threat regardless of source — which is closer to what polyvagal interventions target.

We welcome rigorous examination of both hypotheses. We may be wrong about the mechanism. We are confident the effect is real.

04 — the evidence base

What we know. What we're finding out.

The psychoacoustic mechanism underlying Resonero is grounded in established science. The clinical and communicative outcomes evidence is being built now — through IRB-approved research at UTEP and collaboration with researchers across audiology, organizational behavior, and clinical practice.

A note on intentions, errors, and omissions.

Corrections and insights welcome.

A goal of this website is to present a comprehensible, contextualized description of the perceptual phenomena Resonero interacts with — accurate enough to be useful to curious non-specialists, honest enough to be useful to researchers.

Resonero is being developed by Eric Freudenthal, a university faculty member with an engineering background who recently branched into psychoacoustics. With the help of colleagues and AI tools, he has been studying previously unfamiliar literature — and has surely missed critical nuances, overlooked relevant work, and occasionally drawn inferences the evidence doesn't quite support.

Dr. Freudenthal and his collaborators would very much like to know if something is cited incorrectly, if a finding has been mischaracterized, or if relevant work has been missed — particularly yours. And if you have insights, observations, or research that bear on what's described here, they'd genuinely welcome them.

They'd rather be corrected and in conversation than wrong and isolated.

Contact: efreudenthal@utep.edu or eric.freudenthal@freudensong.com
