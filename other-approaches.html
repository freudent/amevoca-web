— a note on masking

Masking is not a failure. It's often a strategy.

Most people mask their emotional expression to some degree — it's a normative part of social life. We modulate what we show, calibrate to context, and present ourselves differently in different situations. For many people this is effortless. For others, particularly those whose natural expression doesn't land the way they intend, the effort is real and cumulative.

Research on camouflaging — the term used in clinical literature — documents a wide range of individual variation in how effortful this is. Some people adapt so naturally that masking feels like second nature. Others find the sustained monitoring exhausting, with costs to wellbeing that accumulate over time.

If you have a diagnosed condition for which masking has been studied as a clinical strategy, your therapist is the right person to guide you on whether and how to use it. We are not clinicians and we are not offering clinical advice.

Resonero is for a much broader group: people — neurotypical and otherwise — who simply want their conversational voice to carry what they actually feel, without having to work so hard at it.

The three approaches below can each be understood as adaptive strategies of a kind — rational, effective for many people, and worth understanding on their own terms. Resonero is something different: it changes the signal rather than the person's response to it.

01 — polyvagal approaches (e.g. forbrain)

What they do.

Polyvagal-based auditory approaches deliver modulated sound — typically via bone conduction — to engage the nervous system's stress response directly. The proposed mechanism involves the middle ear muscles — the stapedius and tensor tympani — which actively attenuate low-frequency sound when the nervous system is in a socially engaged (ventral vagal) state. Polyvagal interventions attempt to exercise these muscles directly, restoring the regulated state in which low-frequency ambient sounds are naturally suppressed and mid-frequency prosodic voice becomes more perceptible.

People using these approaches report genuine benefit: easier communication, reduced anxiety, improved focus. We take these reports seriously.

How we think IA is different.

Our hypothesis is that the relevant variable isn't low-frequency sound per se — it's low-frequency sound without a source that fits our understanding of a safe environment. Intonation melody can help contextualize and validate a voice as human, emotionally readable, and safe. Its absence may leave conversational voice acoustically anomalous in ways we don't consciously register — but feel.

The broadcast voice observation. The bump in the night is probably universally alarming. Yet the soothing voice of the smooth jazz FM announcer — rich in low frequencies — is, for most of us, not just fine but reassuring. Strip away the intonation melody and it might sound creepy. That contrast is what our hypothesis is built on.

If you are experiencing something more complex or medical in nature, we'd encourage you to seek support from someone with relevant expertise.

02 — speech-language pathology & speech therapy

What it addresses.

Most people associate speech therapy with functional challenges — difficulties that affect intelligibility: articulation, fluency, voice quality, and the mechanics of producing understandable speech. Speech-language pathologists (SLPs) work extensively in this area, with evidence-based, clinician-guided approaches targeting measurable outcomes.

Less commonly recognized but equally important is SLP's role in pragmatic communication — the social use of language. Pragmatics covers how we use speech in context: reading conversational cues, knowing when to speak, adjusting register, interpreting intent, and understanding what's meant beyond what's said. For people whose challenge is in this layer — rather than in the mechanics of producing speech — pragmatic communication therapy is the relevant intervention.

Resonero operates at a related but distinct layer: the acoustic signal that carries prosodic and emotional information. It doesn't teach communication strategies or build vocal technique. It modifies the room's acoustics so that the signal is clearer — to speakers and listeners alike. For some people this may reduce the effort required to apply strategies they've already developed. For others it may be a first step toward understanding what the challenge actually is.

If you're uncertain which layer is most relevant for you, that conversation is worth having with an SLP. They can assess what's actually going on and help you decide what kind of support makes sense.

03 — acting classes & communication training

What they do.

Acting training and communication coaching teach emotion projection, voice control, body language, presence, and strategic communication. They develop a conscious toolkit for expressing yourself effectively.

How training works — and how IA is different.

Like SLP's behavioral strategies, communication training is compensatory: it builds conscious technique layered over natural expression. This can be powerful — the ability to project warmth even when you're nervous, or to hold attention in a large room.

IA operates at a different level. It enables natural emotional expression by providing accurate signal — rather than teaching you to consciously shape your voice toward a target. When the signal is clear, intention comes through naturally. No technique required.

when training might be right

You want to develop a performance skill — presence, projection, persuasion. You enjoy structured practice and community. You want to expand your range consciously.

when Resonero might be right

You don't want to perform — you want to be yourself. You're tired of conscious monitoring in every conversation. You want natural self-expression, not technique.

If you're already trained and still experiencing friction in natural conversation, the signal layer may be what's missing. Resonero addresses that without replacing what training gave you.
